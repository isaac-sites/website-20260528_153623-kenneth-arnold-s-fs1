## Introduction

The most famous detail in the Kinross incident is the reported “merge” of two radar blips shortly before the disappearance of the F-89C Scorpion flown by First Lieutenant Felix Moncla Jr and Second Lieutenant Robert L. Wilson. In many later UFO retellings, this moment became the apparent climax of the case: the interceptor closed on an unidentified target, the two radar returns fused into one, and the jet was supposedly swallowed or destroyed by the unknown object. Yet the actual evidential value of the radar merge is far more limited than popular accounts suggest.

<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Kinross_incident_195_2ebd01_radar_merge_limits_430513-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Radar Merge illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Kinross_incident_195_2ebd01_radar_merge_limits_430513-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Kinross_incident_195_2ebd01_radar_merge_limits_430513-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
The radar data remains important because it confirms that ground controllers were tracking both the interceptor and another aircraft or radar contact over Lake Superior. However, 1950s ground-controlled interception radar had relatively coarse resolution, intermittent tracking quality, and important operational limitations. A merged return could mean two aircraft were extremely close together on the radar display, but it did not by itself prove collision, contact, or disappearance into another object. Understanding those technical limits is central to understanding why the Kinross incident remains unresolved rather than conclusively mysterious.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.openskiesproject.org/news/kinross-incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openskiesproject.org">[openskiesproject.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openskiesproject.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Kinross IncidentDuring that era, radar resolution was relatively low, causing targets in close proximity to merge on the radar screen...</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground-controlled_interception" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ground-controlled interception</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ground-controlled interception</span></span></span>

## What Ground Radar Reportedly Showed

According to later reconstructions based on surviving Air Force material and radar-station history, controllers at Calumet Air Force Station tracked an unidentified or insufficiently identified aircraft over Lake Superior on the evening of 23 November 1953. The target was later officially associated with a Royal Canadian Air Force C-47 transport that was reportedly off course. An F-89C interceptor, callsign Avenger Red, was scrambled from Kinross Air Force Base to identify it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.openskiesproject.org/news/gci" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openskiesproject.org">[openskiesproject.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openskiesproject.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ground-Controlled InterceptionThis allowed a single radar station to provide a complete 360 degree view of the airspace around the statio...</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Moncla" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Felix MonclaThe USAF reported that Moncla had crashed into Lake Superior while tracking a Royal Canadian Air Force (RCAF) C-47 aircraf...</span></span></span>

Ground-control operators then guided the interceptor toward the target using Cold War-era ground-controlled interception procedures. Near the end of the intercept sequence, radar operators reportedly observed the two returns converge into a single radar blip. Soon afterwards, the F-89’s Identification Friend or Foe signal disappeared and radio contact was lost. Search operations later failed to recover the aircraft or crew.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.visitkeweenaw.com/blog/post/missing-in-the-keweenaw-the-kinross-incident/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitkeweenaw.com">[2visitkeweenaw.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitkeweenaw.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Missing in the Keweenaw &#124; The Kinross Incident20 Nov 2023 — The two aircraft appeared to converge on the control screen at Calumet, then...</span></span></span>

This sequence created two very different narratives:

* In the official accident interpretation, the merge represented the interceptor successfully closing with the target before suffering an unrelated accident.
* In later UFO literature, the merge became interpreted as evidence that the unknown object physically absorbed, destroyed, or abducted the interceptor.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://science.howstuffworks.com/space/aliens-ufos/ufo-government4.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.howstuffworks.com">[HowStuffWorks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.howstuffworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo government4</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The 1953 Kinross UFO IncidentRadar operators watched the aircraft close in on the UFO, and then something fantastic happened...</span></span></span>

The problem is that the radar evidence itself cannot reliably distinguish between those interpretations.

## Why Two Targets Could Appear as One

### Radar Resolution in the Early 1950s

The Kinross intercept occurred during an era when military radar was effective for broad tracking but comparatively crude in fine detail. Ground-controlled interception systems were designed primarily to place fighter aircraft near a target, not to produce forensic-quality positional data. Operators viewed sweeping radar displays in which nearby targets could blur together depending on distance, angle, altitude separation, weather interference, and display quality.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.raf.mod.uk/our-organisation/stations/raf-boulmer/news/80th-anniversary-of-the-first-ground-control-intercept-radar-site/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: raf.mod.uk">[Royal Air Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">raf.mod.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Royal Air Force80th Anniversary of the First Ground Control Intercept...1 Jan 2021 — Three advances combined to solve the night-time int...</span></span></span> Wikipedia The Open Skies Project’s technical discussion of the Kinross case directly addresses this issue<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground-controlled_interception" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ground-controlled interception</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ground-controlled interception</span></span></span>, noting that radar systems of the period had low enough resolution that close targets commonly merged into one display return. The project specifically argues that later UFO writers misunderstood this behaviour and treated a normal radar effect as evidence of physical contact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.openskiesproject.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openskiesproject.org">[openskiesproject.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openskiesproject.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open Skies ProjectOur goal is to preserve and display the history of this legendary Cold War radar station and its place in the local his...</span></span></span>

Modern radar terminology still uses the idea of a “merge” or “merge plot” to describe tracks entering the same radar resolution cell. Contemporary interception doctrine recognises that when two aircraft come within the radar system’s discrimination limits, controllers may temporarily see only one combined return.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://wiki.ivao.aero/en/home/specialoperations/so-training-documentation/SpecialOperationsFlying/GCI" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wiki.ivao.aero">[IVAO Documentation Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wiki.ivao.aero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Radar returns/tracks have come together. ICs will refer to fighters in MERGED state when the system&#x27;s resolution capability is limited or...</span></span></span>

In practical terms, this means the Kinross merge could indicate several possibilities:

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* The interceptor had approached very closely to the target.
* The radar lacked enough precision to display two nearby returns separately.
* One track temporarily masked or overlapped the other on the display.
* Weather, signal fading, or operator interpretation affected the presentation.

</div>

None of those possibilities automatically implies collision.

### The Limits of Position Accuracy

Another weakness in later UFO retellings is the assumption that radar displays showed exact physical relationships between aircraft. In reality, 1950s ground radar often produced approximate positional information rather than precise spatial certainty. Operators were working with rotating sweeps, manually interpreted returns, and systems vulnerable to clutter, propagation effects, and intermittent fading.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://marconiradarhistory.pbworks.com/w/file/fetch/139431675/MWT%20Report%201949%20-%20RAF%20Ground%20Control%20Interception%20System%20of%20RAF.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: marconiradarhistory.pbworks.com">[2marconiradarhistory.pbworks.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">marconiradarhistory.pbworks.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tab RADAR GROUND CONTROL INTERCEPTION...Develop a method whereby information from any two at any time of the following equipments, 12 metre...</span></span></span>

Altitude separation was also difficult to judge from many radar displays of the period. Two aircraft at substantially different heights could still appear merged on a plan-position display if their horizontal positions overlapped from the radar’s perspective. A merged blip therefore did not necessarily mean the aircraft occupied the same physical space.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMES_Type_80" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AMES Type 80</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AMES Type 80</span></span></span>

This matters because many dramatic retellings implicitly assume that the radar operators witnessed a literal impact or disappearance event. The surviving descriptions do not support that level of certainty.

## What the Merge Did and Did Not Prove

The radar merge remains meaningful evidence, but its evidential strength is narrower than often claimed.

What the merge strongly suggests:

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The F-89 was successfully vectored toward another radar target.
* The interceptor likely came very close to that target.
* Ground controllers were actively tracking the intercept sequence in real time.
* The disappearance occurred during or immediately after the closing phase of the intercept.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.visitkeweenaw.com/blog/post/missing-in-the-keweenaw-the-kinross-incident/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: visitkeweenaw.com">[2visitkeweenaw.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">visitkeweenaw.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Missing in the Keweenaw &#124; The Kinross Incident20 Nov 2023 — The two aircraft appeared to converge on the control screen at Calumet, then...</span></span></span>

</div>

What the merge does not prove:

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* That the two aircraft collided.
* That the unknown target destroyed the interceptor.
* That the target was extraterrestrial or anomalous.
* That the radar operators saw a single object absorb another object.
* That only one object left the area afterwards.

</div>

Some later accounts even claimed that the combined blip continued flying away after the merge, implying that the unknown craft survived while the fighter vanished. Surviving documentation for that claim is weak and inconsistent. The more technically grounded reconstructions instead describe loss of the interceptor’s return and communication rather than a clearly tracked post-merge escape by a UFO.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.mibsar.com/Cases/PukaskwaPlaneWreck/USAFcrashReport.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mibsar.com">[2mibsar.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mibsar.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">USAFcrash Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">REPORT OF AIRCRAFT ACCIDENT Use this form in...24 Nov 2025 — The radar return from the other aircraft indicated it was continuing on its...</span></span></span>

## How the Merge Fed Later UFO Claims

### The Influence of UFO Writers

The merged-blip story became central to UFO mythology partly because it condensed the case into a dramatic visual image: two radar targets become one and then vanish. Writers such as Donald Keyhoe helped popularise this interpretation during a period when radar evidence carried enormous cultural authority. Radar was widely viewed by the public as objective and nearly infallible, especially in Cold War air-defence settings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.openskiesproject.org/news/kinross-incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openskiesproject.org">[openskiesproject.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openskiesproject.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Kinross IncidentDuring that era, radar resolution was relatively low, causing targets in close proximity to merge on the radar screen...</span></span></span>

## The Most Cautious Reading of the Radar Evidence

A careful reading of the Kinross radar evidence leads to a narrower but still significant conclusion. The radar operators almost certainly tracked an intercept in progress, and the interceptor probably closed to near visual range of another aircraft or radar contact over Lake Superior. Shortly afterwards, the F-89 disappeared from communications and radar tracking.

Beyond that point, the radar evidence becomes much less decisive.

The merge itself was consistent with ordinary radar behaviour for closely spaced targets on 1950s ground-controlled interception systems. It does not independently demonstrate collision, hostile action, or extraordinary technology. At the same time, it remains important because it establishes timing and sequence: the disappearance happened during the intercept phase rather than randomly elsewhere in flight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.openskiesproject.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: openskiesproject.org">[openskiesproject.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">openskiesproject.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open Skies ProjectOur goal is to preserve and display the history of this legendary Cold War radar station and its place in the local his...</span></span></span><span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Moncla" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Felix MonclaThe USAF reported that Moncla had crashed into Lake Superior while tracking a Royal Canadian Air Force (RCAF) C-47 aircraf...</span></span></span>
