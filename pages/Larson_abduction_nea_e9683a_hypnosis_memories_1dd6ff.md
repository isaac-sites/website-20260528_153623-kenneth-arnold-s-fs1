## Introduction

In many UFO abduction reports, the most dramatic and detailed elements — including beings, medical procedures, and being taken aboard a craft — emerge **only after hypnotic regression** rather than in the witness’s initial account. The 1975 Larson abduction near Fargo is no exception: the vivid “missing hour” narrative and imagery largely come from hypnosis sessions conducted later, and this raises core questions about the **reliability of recovered memories**. This page focuses tightly on *how hypnosis was used in this case*, what the hypnotically recovered material contained, and **why memory researchers and many psychologists dispute that such hypnosis reliably retrieves real events**.

## R. Leo Sprinkle and Regression Sessions in the Larson Case

In the decades after the Larson incident, psychologist **R. Leo Sprinkle** — a researcher associated with UFO abduction studies — worked with Sandy Larson and her daughter Jackie to explore what they could not consciously recall about the period of “missing time.” According to published narratives of the case, this regression involved relaxed-state questioning intended to access memories that were not available to Larson in ordinary recall. Under hypnosis, **Sandy reportedly described being floated into a craft, examined by non-human beings, and subjected to physical procedures** — details that were not part of the original contemporaneous account of the sighting and missing time. Her daughter’s hypnotic report similarly placed her outside the vehicle and immobilised during this period. These hypnotically recovered elements form the core of the abduction narrative in the case, since **no physical evidence or contemporaneous detailed testimony exists to corroborate them**.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.tall-white-aliens.com/the-1975-north-dakota-ufo-sighting/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tall-white-aliens.com">[Tall-White Aliens]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tall-white-aliens.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mrs. Larson, who lived in Fargo, North Dakota, was planning to take a real estate test in Bismarck, 200 miles away. &#124; &quot;Tall-White&quot; aliens...</span></span></span>

Sprinkle’s work, and his broader involvement in the abduction research community, helped to **establish hypnosis as a commonplace tool among UFO investigators** for “recovering missing time” and reconstructing abductee narratives when memory gaps occur. His perspective was that many experiencers had little conscious recall of key portions of their incidents and that hypnosis could assist in retrieval. However, this approach is controversial even within ufology and is viewed with scepticism by mainstream psychology.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.mydarkpath.com/81-dr-leo-sprinkle" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mydarkpath.com">[My Dark Path]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mydarkpath.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

## Medical-Exam Imagery and Entity Descriptions Under Hypnosis

What makes the hypnotic part of the Larson account especially vivid are the **specific sensory and procedural details** that emerged: a robot‑like entity with striking features, medical‑style procedures involving liquid and instruments, and a sense of sensory and emotional intensity during the experience. This pattern — detailed narrative produced only under trance — is common in many hypnosis‑based abduction reports and highlights why hypnosis is so central to the way the abduction story developed in this case. In the absence of independently recorded testimony from the time of the event, the hypnosis‑derived sequence becomes the most elaborate source of information about the alleged abduction.

Yet, while compelling as narrative, these details are not supported by physical trace evidence or multiple independent retrospective sources. For the Larson case, **the only person who declined deeper follow‑up (Terry O’Leary) did not contribute a detailed public statement** under hypnosis, leaving the hypnotically recovered elements largely dependent on the testimony of Larson and her daughter.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.tall-white-aliens.com/the-1975-north-dakota-ufo-sighting/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tall-white-aliens.com">[Tall-White Aliens]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tall-white-aliens.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mrs. Larson, who lived in Fargo, North Dakota, was planning to take a real estate test in Bismarck, 200 miles away. &#124; &quot;Tall-White&quot; aliens...</span></span></span>

## Why Recovered Memories Are Disputed

### Suggestibility and Memory Reconstruction

Mainstream memory research cautions that **hypnosis does not act like a truth serum** and is not a reliable tool for retrieving accurate lost memories. Numerous studies and reviews indicate that hypnosis often increases a person’s *confidence* in their memories and *the amount of detail retrieved*, but this **does not guarantee accuracy**; in fact, it can also increase the likelihood of confabulation. Hypnotised individuals become more suggestible and may integrate cues from the hypnotist, cultural expectations, or their own beliefs into the narrative they produce.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perspectives_on_the_alien_abduction_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Perspectives on the alien abduction phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Perspectives on the alien abduction phenomenon</span></span></span>

Analyses of false memory phenomena — in both alien abduction contexts and other domains like supposed recovered memories of childhood trauma — show that **memory is reconstructive** and can be shaped by suggestion, questioning style, and the social context of the retrieval session. Hypnotic regression can inadvertently legitimise imagined scenarios as “real memories,” especially when the subject and the facilitator share an interpretive frame that favours UFO abduction interpretations.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.nspb.net/index.php/nspb/article/view/288" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nspb.net">[NSPB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nspb.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The -Files: A Review of the Psychological Literature Regarding False Memories of Alien Abduction &#124; The New School Psychology Bulleti...</span></span></span>

### Psychological Research on Hypnosis and Abduction Reports

Empirical work looking at how hypnosis relates to abductee experiences finds that people with pre‑existing beliefs or cognitive styles prone to fantasy are more likely both to seek out hypnosis and to embellish or construct narratives under suggestion. Controlled studies have shown that **suggestions resembling popular abduction themes can be incorporated into hypnotic narratives**, even from individuals without any actual experience of such events, and trained raters often cannot reliably distinguish between genuine and suggested narratives produced under hypnosis.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://spectrum.library.concordia.ca/525/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: spectrum.library.concordia.ca">[Spectrum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">spectrum.library.concordia.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Psychological correlates of the UFO abduction experience: the role of beliefs and indirect suggestions on abduction accounts obt...</span></span></span>

Similarly, broader psychological reviews highlight that abduction memories obtained under hypnosis are **readily shaped by cultural scripts, expectations, and indirect suggestion**. This does not necessarily imply deliberate deception, but it *does challenge the assumption that the content accurately reflects an objective event rather than a psychologically constructed narrative with internal consistency.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.nspb.net/index.php/nspb/article/view/288" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nspb.net">[NSPB]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nspb.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The -Files: A Review of the Psychological Literature Regarding False Memories of Alien Abduction &#124; The New School Psychology Bulleti...</span></span></span>*

### Skeptical Perspectives Within and Outside Ufology

Some UFO researchers themselves — and certainly many psychologists outside the abduction research community — argue that because hypnosis can increase both true and false recollections, **its use should be evaluated with caution or abandoned for event reconstruction**. Critics point out that hypnotic regression was historically tied to the broader “recovered memory” movement in psychotherapy, which produced many contested or demonstrably false memories of childhood events in other contexts. This history serves as a cautionary parallel for interpreting hypnotically retrieved abduction accounts.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perspectives_on_the_alien_abduction_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Perspectives on the alien abduction phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Perspectives on the alien abduction phenomenon</span></span></span>

In sceptical literature, hypnosis in abduction research is often framed not as a direct window into an objective past, but as a **co‑constructed narrative between hypnotist and subject** that reflects the subject’s beliefs, cultural context, and expectations, and should not be taken as independent corroboration of extraordinary physical events.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perspectives_on_the_alien_abduction_phenomenon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Perspectives on the alien abduction phenomenon</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Perspectives on the alien abduction phenomenon</span></span></span>

## Implications for the Larson Abduction Narrative

The Larson case illustrates how hypnosis can transform an ambiguous experience with missing time and unusual sensations into a **richly detailed abductive narrative**. These hypnotically recovered memories have become a central part of how the incident is remembered and retold in UFO literature. However, the **lack of corroborating contemporaneous testimony, physical traces, or independent witness reports from before regression** means that the hypnotic elements should be treated as intriguing narrative data, not as verified evidence of an actual extraterrestrial encounter.

In evaluating the sub‑branch of the Larson abduction focused on hypnosis and recovered memories, it matters that the most powerful and specific claims arise through a method that is contested in both mainstream psychology and within parts of the UFO research community itself. A nuanced assessment recognises that hypnosis reveals *what the subject believes or imagines is likely to have happened*, but it cannot by itself establish that an extraordinary event objectively did occur.
